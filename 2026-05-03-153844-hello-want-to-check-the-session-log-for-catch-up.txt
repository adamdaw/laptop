● Here's what the searches turned up, prioritised by impact:

  ---
  Laptop script

  1. stow --adopt — latent bug. The script runs git config --global (writes real
   files to ~/.gitconfig), then tries to stow the git package over it. Without
  --adopt, stow silently fails on that conflict — the || true suppresses the
  error. The git dotfiles probably aren't actually being stowed. --adopt moves
  the existing file into the stow directory and replaces it with a symlink.
  2. Ghostty isn't installed. The script doesn't install your terminal. On
  Pop_OS (no snaps), the recommended path is the mkasberg/ghostty-ubuntu PPA
  installer.
  3. NVM version is hardcoded (v0.40.3). Becomes stale silently. Could fetch the
   latest tag instead.

  zshrc

  4. fnm instead of NVM — biggest startup win. fnm is ~15ms vs NVM's ~75ms,
  reads .nvmrc natively. Would need a change to both the laptop script (install
  fnm, not NVM) and zshrc (replace the lazy-load block with eval "$(fnm env 
  --use-on-cd)").
  5. Compinit caching — rebuild only once per 24h instead of every shell start.
  ~200–500ms saving.
  6. History setopts — a handful of additional options (HIST_IGNORE_ALL_DUPS,
  HIST_EXPIRE_DUPS_FIRST, EXTENDED_HISTORY, INC_APPEND_HISTORY) for better
  deduplication. No startup cost.
  7. eval "$(fzf --zsh)" — replaces the manual key-bindings file sourcing with
  fzf's own init (works on fzf 0.48+, which apt on 24.04 may or may not have).
  Minor.

  ---
  The stow --adopt issue is the only one that's actually broken. The fnm swap is
   the highest-value improvement. Want to tackle any of these?

● Good one. The core finding is that a market of AI models — bidding on tasks,
  winning via reputation — beats a hub-spoke hierarchy on quality and cost for
  most task types. Solo wins on coding (requires coherent global state), but
  markets dominate reasoning tasks (7.1 vs 5.1) and synthesis. The paradox the
  author highlights: models are catastrophically bad at self-assessment, yet
  markets still outperform hierarchy. The reason is that bad bidding doesn't
  matter much — what matters is that failure triggers a retry from a different
  model with different context accumulation. The diversity premium is the
  product, not accurate routing.

  The Hayek framing is the most interesting part. Coase's question was "why do
  firms exist?" — because internal coordination is sometimes cheaper than market
   transaction costs. Hayek's answer to central planning was that distributed
  price signals aggregate knowledge no central planner can hold. The author is
  saying the same dynamic applies to AI orchestration: a hub-spoke orchestrator
  has to solve two hard problems (decompose the task, recombine the results),
  and when decomposition isn't obvious, it burns its advantage figuring that
  out. Markets sidestep both by just letting attempts compete.

  The pull quote does the work: "Markets beat managers when the value of 
  independent retry exceeds the value of orchestrated coherence."

  Two things I'd push back on or want to see explored further: (1) the
  experiment is 15 hand-written tasks — small enough that the variance is
  probably large and task selection could flip the results. (2) The coding
  finding (solo/hub-spoke win) is described as "requires one continuous line of
  thought," but that's also the description of most of the hardest real
  engineering tasks. So the market advantage may be concentrated in exactly the
  tasks that are less consequential.

  Relevant to your Daedalus article: VSDD is essentially hub-spoke (Claude as
  orchestrator, phases as workers), and by this framework it should excel at
  coherent decomposition tasks — which is exactly what it does. The article
  gives you a Hayek-flavoured theoretical backing for why that works, and also
  flags where it breaks down (routing cost when the problem structure isn't
  obvious up front).

  Worth a library card? It's a good reference for the agent coordination design
  space.

● Shorter piece, but there's a clean inversion at the centre of it: most
  monitoring setups go metrics-first (here's CPU/memory/latency, let's set
  thresholds), which produces noisy systems. This document says start from
  failures — "what service breakdowns actually affect users?" — and work
  backward to the metrics that predict those. Everything else follows from that:
   thresholds are calibrated to real failure modes, false alarms are
  structurally less likely, and when they do occur you have a clear removal
  criterion ("if no action can or should be taken, the alert is not needed").

  The "zero tolerance for false alarms" principle is strong and I largely agree
  with it for web services and developer tooling. The caveat is that it's
  context-dependent — in safety-critical or financial infrastructure you'd

  flips. For Unitasker or a homelab server, zero-tolerance is exactly right.

   ongoing maintenance. That's the same insight as "tests are first-class
  citizens" — monitoring debt accumulates the same way technical debt does, and

● That connection holds up well. Alert-driven monitoring is just event-driven
  architecture applied to observability — meaningful state changes produce
  events, consumers react. The epistemology is identical: stop polling, stop
  watching dashboards, let the system surface what matters when it matters.

  The 2026 angle you're pointing at is sharp. When autonomous agents are
  consumers on that event bus, the "zero tolerance for false alarms" principle
  stops being a developer-experience concern and becomes a correctness concern.
  A false alarm that pages a human is annoying. A false alarm that triggers an
  agent to restart a service, roll back a deployment, or escalate a ticket is a
  different category of problem — the agent acts, and now you have downstream
  consequences to unwind. The signal quality requirement goes up as the consumer
   autonomy goes up.

  The other thing that gets more important with agents is event schema
  discipline. In a human-on-call model you can tolerate loosely structured
  alerts because a human reads the context and infers what to do. An agent needs
   a well-defined event shape to route and act reliably — which pushes toward
  something like a proper event contract or schema registry, not just "send a
  Slack message with some fields." That's the gap between "we have alerting" and
   "we have a monitoring system agents can use."

       60 +## Key terms — _analytical_                                   
       61 +                                                              
       62 +**Affordance:** The perceived and actual properties of an obje
          +ct that determine how it could be used. A door handle affords 
          +pulling; a flat plate affords pushing. The 2013 edition splits
          + this into affordances (actual properties) and signifiers (sig
          +nals that communicate where and how to act) — the held origina
          +l uses "affordance" for both.                                 
       63 +                                                              
       64 +**Conceptual model:** The user's mental model of how a system 
          +works. Good design makes the designer's model and the user's m
          +odel converge.                                                
       65 +                                                              
       66 +**Mapping:** The relationship between controls and their effec
          +ts. Natural mapping (left control moves things left) reduces m
          +emory load. Arbitrary mapping produces errors.                
       67 +                                                              
       68 +**Feedback:** Communication of the results of an action. Witho
          +ut feedback, users cannot tell whether their action succeeded,
          + failed, or is still processing.                              
       69 +                                                              
       70 +**Constraints:** Physical, logical, semantic, and cultural lim
          +itations that guide correct action.                           
       71 +                                                              
       72 +**Gulf of execution / gulf of evaluation:** The gap between wh
          +at a user wants to do and available actions (execution); the g
          +ap between the system's actual state and the user's ability to
          + perceive it (evaluation).                                    
       73 +                                                              
       74 +**Slip vs. mistake:** A slip is the right intention executed w
          +rongly. A mistake is the wrong intention — incorrect conceptua
          +l model. These require different design interventions.

       77 +
